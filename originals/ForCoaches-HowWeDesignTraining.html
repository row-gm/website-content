Coaching at ROW

How We Design Training

What ROW believes about training, and the words we all use to describe it. Four documents sit underneath this one. They cover the season, the block, the practice, and the swimmer.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

What we believe

Eight principles. Everything in the four layers below follows from these.

1

Aerobic development comes first

At every age, in every group. The aerobic base is what everything else is built on, and it is the one thing that cannot be rushed later. Fast swimming built on a thin base does not hold up.
2

Adaptation comes from the unload, not the load

Swimmers do not improve during hard training. They improve while recovering from it. Easy weeks are not time off from the plan. They are the part of the plan that pays.
3

Skill is developed under fatigue

Perfect technique when fresh is a starting point, not the goal. Races are swum tired, so skill work belongs inside hard sets and not only in the warm-up.
4

Speed is maintained, never rebuilt

Short, fast work appears in every phase and every group, all year. Speed is lost quickly and costs very little to keep.
5

We prescribe effort, not pace

A zone tells a swimmer how hard to work. It does not tell them what time to hold. That is what lets one set serve a whole lane of different abilities. It also means the rest is usually the thing to fix, not the send-off.
6

Every season adds a layer to the foundation

Each year is trained fully and raced hard, and each year is built on the one before it. The aim is a swimmer whose full potential is there to be used when physical and psychological maturity arrive. That takes patience in the early years and it pays back for the rest of a career.
7

Every year they need us less

Our goal is to graduate athletes who are experts in their own performance. A swimmer who only performs when a coach is watching has not finished developing.
8

A shared language, not a recipe book

The plan is written down so it can be handed over, questioned, and improved. What is shared across ROW is the language and the philosophy. The creativity each coach brings to their own group is the point of the work, not a departure from it.
These principles describe how we train. The ROW Way describes what we are developing. Four dimensions, twelve markers written in the swimmer's own words, and a four-step scale from “learning it” to “when it’s hard”. It is open to every member, and it is what swimmer reviews and group move conversations are built on.

The five zones

The shared language for all four layers. A season, a week, a practice and a single swimmer's lane are all described in these five words.

The number is the name. Zone 3 in writing, Z3 on a board. The colour is kept as a descriptor, because it runs roughly the way a swimmer looks at that effort, but it is not how a zone is called. One handle, so nobody has to translate.

The heart rate bands come from Swimming Canada's training zone terminology, which already works in beats below maximum. Our five zones map onto their categories without forcing, and Zone 3 covers two of them, which is why it is the widest zone on the page.

Descriptor White — Recovery

Beats in 10 seconds 24 to 26
Typical set 400s to 1500s, 10 to 20 sec rest
Feels like Steady and comfortable. Could keep going a long time. Breathing is settled.
Used for Building the engine. The bulk of Accumulation work. Technique held under mild fatigue.
Zone 3 ⚠  Use with care
Descriptor Red — Aerobic Threshold
Heart rate 40 to 20 below max
Beats in 10 seconds 26 to 30
Typical set 50s to 400s, 10 to 30 sec rest
Feels like Comfortably hard through to hard but sustainable. Working, controlled, talking gets short.
Used for The whole high-end aerobic range, and the zone that carries most of our hard work. Set design does the fine tuning inside it: rep length, rest, and target pace.
Zone 4 ⚠  Use with care
Descriptor Purple — Race Pace
Heart rate 20 to 0 below max
Beats in 10 seconds 30 or more
Typical set 25s to 200s, rest 1:1 or more
Feels like Goal race speed. Hard. Needs real rest to repeat it well.
Used for Learning and holding target pace for the swimmer's events. The core of Intensification.
Zone 5 ✓  All ages
Descriptor Green — Speed
Heart rate Not a heart rate measure
Beats in 10 seconds —
Typical set 10m to 25m, rest 30 sec to 2 min
Feels like Maximum. Full effort, full recovery, every repeat as fast as the first.
Used for Top-end speed, starts, turns, breakouts, stroke rate. Used in every phase, all ages.
A zone is an effort, not a time. That holds across ages, and it holds just as strongly inside a single group. A 12&U swimmer and an 18&U national swimmer can both swim a Zone 3 set in the same block. So can a 50 freestyler and an 800 freestyler, in the same lane, on the same clock.
Count for ten seconds and read the number straight off the card. No multiplying, because that is where the mistakes come from. Start the count within two or three seconds of the wall, since the rate falls quickly once a swimmer stops.
Why beats below maximum, and not a percentage or a set of fixed numbers. The heart rate a swimmer reaches at hard aerobic work falls a long way through adolescence, so any chart written for adults sits wrong for a 12&U swimmer. Working from each swimmer's own maximum removes that problem instead of correcting for it. It also means a swimmer's bands still work when they move up a group.

What each group uses

Same five zones everywhere. The mix follows the &Under band, not the stream. A 12&U swimmer in PD3 and a 12&U swimmer in JD2 use the same zones; they differ in how much, not in which. What the stream changes is which meet they peak for.

Do not rush the top of Zone 3, or Zone 4, with young swimmers. Hard anaerobic work buys quick short-term gains and costs long-term development. If a 12&U set leaves swimmers unable to hold technique, it was the wrong zone.
