Goal Setting Tool

A simple way to set your next goal time. Take one best time, do a little math, and you have a target for every other distance, plus the splits to swim it.

The one rule

When you double the distance, you do not just double the time. You slow down a little. This tool builds that slow-down into the number.

Double your time, then add the drop-off. That is the whole idea. The drop-off gets bigger as the race gets longer, because fatigue has more time to build.

The ladder

Each rung uses the goal time from the rung below it, not your personal best. Build up one step at a time.

Step up What you do Formula
50 to 100 Double it, add 5 seconds 50 x 2 + 5
100 to 200 Double it, add 10 seconds 100 x 2 + 10
200 to 400 Double it, add 10 to 15 seconds 200 x 2 + 10 to 15
400 to 800 Double it, add 25 to 30 seconds 400 x 2 + 25 to 30
400 to 1500 Multiply by 4 400 x 4
The 400 goal is a range. Build the 800 and the 1500 from the middle of it. That keeps every range tight enough to aim at. The 1500 gets no add-on at all, so it lands on a single number.

Starting somewhere else

Not everyone is a 50 swimmer. If your best event is the 100 or the 200, you run the same math backwards. Subtract the drop-off first, then halve it. Distance events work the same way and are covered further down.

Your best event is the 100

Goal Formula Maya at 1:05.0
50 (100 time - 5) / 2 30.0
200 100 time x 2 + 10 2:20.0

Your best event is the 200

Goal Formula Maya at 2:20.0
100 (200 time - 10) / 2 1:05.0
50 (100 goal - 5) / 2 30.0
The ladder works in both directions and always lands on the same numbers. A 30.00 fifty, a 1:05.0 hundred and a 2:20.0 two hundred are the same swimmer. That is how you spot which of your events is furthest ahead.

Target splits

A goal time tells you where to finish. Target splits tell you how to get there.

Your first 50 is always the fastest. You dive in and you are fresh, so it comes for free. After that, hold the same time on every 50. Then bring the last 50 home faster: about 0.6 seconds in a 400, and about a second in an 800 or 1500.

How much slower your second 50 is than your first is your drop-off, and it depends on the stroke. Breaststroke and butterfly drop more than freestyle and backstroke.

Splits are rounded to a tenth, so they may add up a few tenths either side of your goal. Swim the numbers, not the total.

The drop-off, checked after the race

Compare your first 50 to your second 50. That gap is your drop-off. Keep it inside these numbers, whatever your finishing time was.

Stroke 100 race 200 and longer
Freestyle and backstroke 2.0 seconds slower 3.0 seconds slower
Breaststroke and butterfly 4.0 seconds slower 4.0 seconds slower

Inside the number means you paced it well, even if you missed the target. Outside it means you went out too fast.

Going out faster than your target first 50 is the most common mistake in a race. It feels good for one length and costs you the back half. Practise the target pace in training until it feels slow.

The medley

In development — testing mode

The 200 IM and the 400 IM do not work like the other events. Your medley time is set by the stroke you are worst at. Two swimmers with the same 200 free can be ten seconds apart in the 200 IM. One best time cannot predict a medley, so you need a time in all four.

Never raced one of the four? Race it first. A medley swimmer needs a time in all four strokes before any of this works.

Your medley goal

Add your best time in each of the four strokes. Halve the total. Then add a few seconds for the turns and for the work of swimming all four back to back.

Medley Times you need Formula
200 IM Best 100 of each stroke (four 100s) / 2 + 6.5 to 8.5
400 IM Best 200 of each stroke (four 200s) / 2 + 5 to 10
Your goal comes out as a range, never a single number. It is built from your own four strokes, so it already allows for the stroke you are weakest at.

A second opinion on the 400 IM

If you have raced a 200 IM, you can check the answer a second way.

400 IM = 200 IM x 2 + 20 to 25

Use it as a check, not as the answer. If the two overlap, your estimate is solid. If they do not, trust the four-stroke number, and read the gap as a sign that your 200 IM and your 400 IM are at different stages.

Your leg targets

Each leg takes a fairly steady share of a medley, whatever the swimmer. Multiply the middle of your goal range by the share for each leg.

Leg Share of a 200 IM Share of a 400 IM
Butterfly 21.78% 22.84%
Backstroke 25.38% 25.67%
Breaststroke 29.13% 28.49%
Freestyle 23.71% 23.00%
The two columns are not the same. Butterfly takes a bigger share of a 400 IM than of a 200 IM, and breaststroke takes a smaller one, so use the column for your race. Breaststroke takes the largest share in both races only because it is the slowest stroke, not because the race is won there.

Which stroke is holding you back?

Work out what share of your own four-stroke total each stroke takes, then compare it with the benchmark below. A share bigger than the benchmark means that stroke is slow for you. It does not matter how fast you are overall, because it is a comparison with yourself.

Stroke Share of your four 100s Share of your four 200s
Butterfly 24.48% 25.12%
Backstroke 24.65% 24.52%
Breaststroke 28.11% 28.27%
Freestyle 22.76% 22.09%

Within about 0.4 of a percentage point either way is balanced. More than that and the stroke is worth a closer look.

Use this table for the balance check only. The leg target shares further up are different numbers, because a flat 100 butterfly sits further behind a flat 100 free than a butterfly leg sits behind a free leg inside a medley.
If your backstroke or breaststroke reads slow, start there. Across 1,095 elite swimmers, backstroke and breaststroke moved medley results the most, and butterfly the least. That is the opposite of how it feels. Butterfly comes first and hurts most, so it gets the attention, and it is the leg where extra work pays back least.

How to approach your goals

The ladder gives you six numbers. It does not tell you which one to chase. Work through these three questions in order.

1

Are you versatile?

Run the ladder from your best sprint, then run it again from your best distance event. If the two agree, your range is balanced. If one event lands well ahead of what the other predicts, that event is your real strength and the ladder should be rebuilt from it.
Ahead of the goal = strength
Well behind the goal = where to train
2

Pick your next-phase goals

Chase what is close. A goal within about 2% of your current time is reachable in one training block. Pick one or two, not six. Six goals is no goals.
2% of 1:05.0 = 1.3 seconds
2% of 4:52.5 = 5.9 seconds
3

Set one stretch goal for the season

Something within about 5% is a season target. Further out than that is a longer project. Name it if you want to, but do not judge your season on it.
5% of 1:05.0 = 3.3 seconds
5% of 4:52.5 = 14.6 seconds
The 2% and 5% bands start the conversation, they do not end it. A 10&U swimmer improves faster than an 18&U swimmer, and a swimmer new to an event improves faster than one who has raced it for years. Bring your numbers to your coach.

If your best event is the 400, 800 or 1500

You run the ladder backwards. Each step up adds a range, so each step down subtracts the middle of that range. The chain lands on single numbers and returns exactly to the time you entered.

Your best event Work back to Formula
400 200 goal (PB400 - 12.5) / 2
800 400 goal (PB800 - 27.5) / 2
1500 400 goal PB1500 / 4

From your 200 goal, keep going down: the 100 goal is (200 goal minus 10) divided by 2, and the 50 goal is (100 goal minus 5) divided by 2.

Distance swimmers should expect their sprint goals to look soft, and that is real, not a flaw in the math. The world's best 1500 swimmers are better over 1500 relative to their 400 than the ladder assumes. If you are a distance swimmer, the 50 and 100 goals are a measure of the speed you are missing, not a target you should already be hitting.

Common questions

Are these goals or predictions?
They are goals. The math gives you a fair target based on speed you have already shown. Training, pacing and race experience decide whether you hit it.
What if I am already faster than my goal time?
Good news. It means that event is ahead of the rest of your range. Rebuild the ladder using that faster time as your new starting point.
Does this work for short course and long course?
Run the math within one course. Use a short course best time to set short course goals, and a long course best time to set long course goals. Do not mix the two.
Why is there a range on the 400 and 800?
Longer races leave more room for pacing differences. The low end suits swimmers with strong endurance. The high end suits swimmers whose speed is ahead of their aerobic base.
My best event is the 400 or 800. What then?
Work backwards to a 200 goal first, then use the 200 column. Ask your coach to help set that starting number.
Does stroke change my goal times?
No. The 50, 100 and 200 math is the same in every stroke. Stroke only changes your split targets, because breaststroke and butterfly fade more than freestyle and backstroke over the first 50.
My splits are inside the standard but I missed my goal time.
Then your opening 50 was too slow. Splits and goal times work together. Check that your first 50 sat inside the opening range for your goal.
Should I use this instead of my coach's goals?
No. Use it as a starting point for the conversation. Your coach sees your training, your stroke and your race plan, and their target wins.
