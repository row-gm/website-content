The Growth Spurt

Every swimmer grows at their own time. Understanding when and what it does to swimming makes the next few years easier for everyone.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

how growth happens

If you've ever been to a graduation ceremony for elementary or high school, you've seen the human development science firsthand. Growth is not linear. There are significant differences in timing that impact maturity and performance.

Same class, three points in time. By university, the differences have almost disappeared.

By the last row, everyone has finished growing and the gap has closed. Between the first row and the last, races are being won and lost by biology.

What a growth spurt is

Somewhere in your teens you grow faster than at any time since you were a baby. Coaches call the fastest point peak height velocity. We just call it the growth spurt.

1

It happens at very different ages

On average it comes around 12 for girls and around 14 for boys. Average is not a rule. Two years either side is completely normal.
2

Arms and legs grow first

Hands, feet and limbs lengthen before the body catches up. You are suddenly a different shape in the water.
3

Strength arrives later

You get longer before you get stronger. The strength catches up, but not at the same time.
Nobody chooses when it happens. It is not something you earn by training harder, and it is not something you can hurry along.

What it does to your swimming

A growth spurt can make swimming feel worse for a while. This surprises people, so it is worth saying plainly.

Your feel for the water changes. Longer arms move through the water differently. A stroke that worked last season can feel clumsy.

Your timing goes off. Turns, starts and stroke rhythm were tuned to a body you no longer have.

Your times can stall or go backwards. For a few months, you might train well and swim slower. This is normal and it passes.

If your times stop improving during a growth spurt, that is not a sign you are working less hard or that you have hit your limit. It is a sign your body is busy.

Why 12 year old results can mislead

At 12, a swimmer who grew early is racing against swimmers who have not started yet. They are bigger, stronger and further along. They often win.

That advantage is real, and it is also temporary. When everyone finishes growing, it disappears. Some swimmers who won everything at 12 are mid-pack at 17. Some who never made a final at 12 are the fastest in the club at 17.

Two swimmers, one early and one late. At 12 they look very different. By 18 they do not.
This cuts both ways, and both halves matter. If you are winning now, your speed is real, but some of your lead is timing and it will not last. Keep building skills. If you are behind now, you are not out of the race. You have not started yours yet.

What to do about it

If you are a swimmer

Judge yourself against yourself. Your own times, your own skills, your own effort. Not the tallest person in your lane.

Work on skills while you grow. Turns, starts, streamline, underwaters and pacing do not depend on being big. They are the things you will still own when everyone catches up.

Tell your coach how you feel. Sore knees, tight backs and heavy legs are common during a spurt. Say something.

If you are a parent

Expect a flat patch and do not panic. A season with few best times during a growth spurt is not a wasted season.

Praise the things they control. Attendance, effort, attitude and skills. Not the finishing position.

Watch for growing pains and fatigue. Growing takes energy. Sleep and food matter more during this period, not less.

If you are a coach

Track height, not just times. A sudden jump explains a lot about a swimmer's month.

Protect technique through the spurt. This is when a stroke can drift without anyone noticing.

Do not read early results as potential. Group moves and expectations should account for where a swimmer is in their growth, not just where they finished.

Common questions

How do I know if I am in a growth spurt?
The usual signs are shoes that stop fitting, trousers getting short quickly, and feeling clumsy or sore. Your coach may also track your height through the season.
How long does it last?
The fastest part usually lasts about a year, and the awkward feeling in the water is often shorter than that. Growth continues more slowly for a few years after.
Should I train less while I am growing?
No. You keep training. What may change is the emphasis, with more attention to skills and technique while your body settles. Your coach will guide this.
My times got worse. Am I doing something wrong?
Probably not. A flat or backwards patch during a growth spurt is one of the most normal things in swimming. Keep coming, keep working on skills.
Does growing early mean I will be a better swimmer?
No. It means you got there first. The swimmers around you are still coming.
Does growing late mean I have missed my chance?
No. Late developers often end up with better technique, because they had to learn to swim well before they could rely on being strong.
