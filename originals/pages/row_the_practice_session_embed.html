The Practice Session

How a single session is built and written. One shape, one purpose, and one way to put a set on the board, so a swimmer can read any ROW whiteboard.

The one rule

Every session has one purpose. Name it before you write it. If you cannot say in one sentence what a session is for, the swimmers will not know either, and the set will drift towards moderate work that trains nothing well.

A session also develops the swimmer, not only the swim. What that looks like in practice is at the bottom of this page.

The five zones and the age guidance behind them are on How We Design Training. This document assumes you already have the session's zone from your weekly plan.

Session template

A shape, not a straitjacket. Percentages are of total session distance, and assume a session of 90 minutes or more. Shorter sessions cut the pre-set and shorten the warm-up first.

PartShareWhat it does
PartWarm-upShare15 to 20%What it doesRaise heart rate, open the shoulders and hips, and get the feel of the water. Ends faster than it starts. Mostly Zone 1 and Zone 2.
PartSkill focusShare10 to 15%What it doesOne technical point per session, stated out loud. Drills, then the same point held while swimming. Low speed, high attention.
PartPre-setShare10%What it doesBridges the warm-up and the main set. Builds towards the zone the main set will use, so the first repeat is not the throwaway one.
PartMain setShare35 to 45%What it doesThe reason the session exists. One zone, or two that support each other. This is the part you would protect if the session got cut short.
PartSpeed or race skillsShare10%What it doesStarts, turns, breakouts, finishes, or short Zone 5 work. Do it before fatigue spoils the execution, or keep it deliberately short at the end.
PartCool-downShare10%What it doesGenuine easy swimming, not a token 100. Longer after Zone 3 and Zone 4 sets than after Zone 2.
One main set per session. Two big main sets means neither gets done properly. If the plan needs two, one of them belongs in a different session.

How to write a set

One format, every deck. Reps, distance, interval, zone, focus. The interval is either a send-off (@ 1:30) or a rest interval (:15 rest). Both are written the same way.

8 x 100 @ 1:30 · Z3 · hold 14 strokes
10 x 200 :15 rest · Z2 · even pace, breathe every 5 on the last 50
6 x 50 @ 1:30 · Z4 · 200 goal pace, breathe every 3
10 x 25 from push @ 1:00 · Z5 · max speed, 5 dolphin kicks off the wall

The zone tells the swimmer how hard. The focus tells them what to think about. A set with no focus is a set the swimmer will just get through.

Write one interval on the board, then adjust by lane or by swimmer. Fitting the set to the individual is on The Individual Swimmer, and it does not change what is written here.

Rest interval or send-off

A send-off looks more organized. It is often less fair. On a shared interval the fastest swimmer earns rest and the slowest is punished for being slow, and the two are no longer doing the same set.

Swimmer A gets four times the rest of Swimmer C. A is doing a comfortable Zone 3 set. C is working at the top of Zone 3 and will fade before the end. The board said Zone 3. Only one of them got Zone 3.

8 x 100 :15 rest · Z3

That gives all three the same recovery. They finish at different times, which is the part coaches dislike, and it is a smaller problem than three swimmers doing three different sets.

Keeping it tidy

The organization concern is real. These four fixes handle almost all of it.

1
Regroup at round breaks. Rest interval inside each round, everyone back on the top of the clock between rounds. The drift never grows past one round.
2
Give each lane its own send-off. Same set, three send-offs. Organized like a clock set, fair like a rest set. The middle ground most coaches are looking for.
3
Order the lane by speed. Within a lane, similar swimmers means similar cycles, so the lane stays together on its own.
4
Say the rest out loud each rep early on. Swimmers new to rest intervals need two or three sessions to learn to watch the clock for themselves. It is a skill, and it is worth teaching.

Coaching the whole swimmer

Physical and technical development happen by default in a well-built session. Mental and character development only happen if you build them in. These are the six markers from The ROW Way that a normal session either develops or quietly neglects.

MarkerWhat it looks like on deckWhat it costs you
MarkerStay with the focusWhat it looks like on deckName one focus out loud and put it on the board. At the halfway point, ask two swimmers to say it back to you.What it costs youThirty seconds. If nobody can repeat it, they were never working on it.
MarkerCome back from a setbackWhat it looks like on deckWhen a swimmer misses the interval, the next rep is the reset. Say “next one”, not “that’s fine”. Build one set a week where holding on is not expected.What it costs youNothing. It is a choice of words and a choice of set.
MarkerKnow what I'm aiming atWhat it looks like on deckAt the start of a block, every swimmer names one thing they are chasing. Refer to it by name at least once a week, to that swimmer.What it costs youFive minutes once a block, then a sentence here and there.
MarkerReady to trainWhat it looks like on deckStart on time. Do not re-explain the set for a swimmer who arrived late. Do not wait for the lane that is still talking.What it costs youNothing, and it is the habit that decides whether the other eleven are possible.
MarkerCoachableWhat it looks like on deckGive a correction, then watch the next rep and tell them whether it changed. Every time.What it costs youAttention. If you never check, you have taught them that corrections are optional.
MarkerPart of the teamWhat it looks like on deckThe last swimmer in the lane gets counted in by the others. Equipment away before anyone leaves the deck.What it costs youNothing. It is a standard you hold or quietly drop.
This is not a talk at the end of practice. If developing these needs a speech, they are not embedded. They are habits inside the session, or they are nothing.

One set, four specialties

Swimmers with different events do not need separate sets. Here is one set serving four swimmers in the same lane block.

MAIN SET · 8 x 100 · Z3 · hold even pace
SwimmerWhat they swimWhy
SwimmerDistance freestyleWhat they swim8 x 100 free, 15 sec restWhyHolds even pace across the whole set. The set as written is already their event.
Swimmer200 IMWhat they swim8 x 100 IM order, one stroke per roundWhySame zone, same rest. The stroke rotation adds the demand their event needs.
Swimmer100 butterflyWhat they swim8 x 100 as 50 fly / 50 free, or 8 x 75WhyShortens the fly exposure so the zone is held honestly instead of degrading into survival.
Swimmer50 and 100 freestyleWhat they swim6 x 100 free, more rest, faster targetWhyStill a Zone 3 set. Fewer reps at a higher intensity suits the swimmer without dropping the aerobic work they need.
The zone is fixed. The prescription moves. Interval, rep count, distance, stroke, rest and target pace are all available to you. Change one at a time and for a stated reason.
