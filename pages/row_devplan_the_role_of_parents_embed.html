Our Development Plan

The Role of Parents

Three people make a swimmer. The one out in front changes as they grow.

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

Three corners, three jobs

A swimmer sits inside a triangle. Each corner has a job that nobody else in the triangle can do, and the whole thing works when each one sticks to theirs.

The sides matter as much as the corners. Each one is a line of communication, and each has to stay open for the triangle to hold.

Who leads changes as they grow

The triangle stays the same shape. What moves is which corner is out in front.

The Early Years - The parent leads

ROW Swim Academy, TOPS, Junior Development

Parents run almost everything. Getting to the pool, packing the kit bag, the food, the sleep, the mood in the car. Your swimmer’s job at this stage is to turn up and enjoy it. The coach is teaching skills rather than managing or directing the season.

 

The Middle Years - The coach leads

Age Group Development, PD3, PD2

The training plan starts driving the week, and the coach is the one holding it. This is the hardest shift for a parent, because it means stepping back from the content while staying fully in charge of everything around it. Your swimmer begins owning their own bag, their own effort, and their own honesty about how a session went.

 

The Later Years - The swimmer leads

Senior Development, PD1, National Development
 

Your swimmer talks to their coach directly, sets their own goals, and decides what to do when something is not working. You become transport, food, and the person who is glad to see them whatever the clock said. That is not a smaller role. It is the one that lasts.

The shifts are the point, not a side effect. Our goal is for your swimmer to be an expert in themselves and their own performance. That cannot happen if the parent is still leading at seventeen, and it will not happen by accident at any age.

What to hand over, and roughly when

Ages are a guide, not a rule. Swimmers are ready at different times, and that is normal.

Keeping the lines open

Most difficulty in the triangle comes from a question going to the wrong corner, or from a line going quiet. Neither is hard to fix once you know which is which.

Catch a coach before or after a practice, not during one. A coach on deck is coaching, and a conversation then takes their attention off a lane of swimmers.

The car ride home

It is the most-remembered conversation in youth sport, and the one most often got wrong. A swimmer who has just raced badly already knows. What they need on the way home is not analysis.

The six words that work: I love watching you swim. Then let them lead. If they want to talk about the race they will, and if they do not, the race will still be there tomorrow when the coach goes through it.

If you want a question, make it one of theirs to answer: what went well, and what would you change. Those two hand the thinking back where it belongs.
